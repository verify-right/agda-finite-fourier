# Number-theoretic transforms and polynomial multiplication

A [number-theoretic transform (NTT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Fourier_transform_(general))
is a discrete Fourier transform over a finite field or suitable ring.

## Roots of unity

Work in $\mathbb{F}_{17}$. The number 4 is a primitive fourth root of unity:

| $k$ | $4^k \bmod 17$ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 4 |
| 2 | 16 |
| 3 | 13 |
| 4 | 1 |

These four distinct roots give us four evaluation points.

## Polynomial multiplication

Multiply two polynomials:

$$
f(x)=1+2x,\qquad g(x)=3+x
$$

Their product is $3+7x+2x^2$.

Instead of multiplying coefficients directly, evaluate both
polynomials at the four roots of unity.

| | 1 | 4 | 16 | 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $f$ | 3 | 9 | 16 | 10 |
| $g$ | 4 | 7 | 2 | 16 |
| $fg$ | 12 | 12 | 15 | 7 |

Multiplication now requires just four pointwise multiplications.

## Inverse transform

The forward transform evaluates a polynomial at powers of 4:

$$
\hat f(k)=\sum_{j=0}^{3}f_j4^{kj}
$$

The inverse uses the inverse root $4^{-1}=13$ and
the inverse length $4^{-1}=13$ modulo 17:

$$
f_j=13\sum_{k=0}^{3}\hat f(k)13^{kj}
$$

Applying it to $(12,12,15,7)$ gives $(3,7,2,0)$,
the coefficients of the product.

## Why it matters

The direct transform takes $O(n^2)$ ring operations.
A radix-2 FFT computes it in $O(n\log n)$.

This makes NTTs useful for polynomial multiplication in
post-quantum cryptography.
